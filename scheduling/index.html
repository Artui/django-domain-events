    Scheduled reactions¶

    "Because this committed, that must happen at a set time afterwards": an offer that expires 48 hours after it was made, an invoice reminder a fortnight after the invoice, a newsletter sent at the time chosen when it was scheduled. The usual answer is a beat job that polls every minute, finds nothing almost every time, keeps no record of the work it did and has no retries, dead letters or visibility - and two overlapping runs can do the same work twice.

    Here it is a receiver that runs later. due= on the declaration writes the delivery row when the event fires, inside the caller's transaction, and the relay claims it when it comes due. Everything a delivery already has - attempts, backoff, dead letters, replay, health, the admin - applies to it unchanged, from its due time on.

    from datetime import timedelta

from django.utils import timezone

from django_domain_events import DeliveryContext, NotYet, receiver


@receiver(OfferMade, key="waitlist.send_offer")
def send_offer(evt: OfferMade) -> None:
    mail_offer(evt.offer_id)


@receiver(OfferMade, key="waitlist.expire_offer", due=timedelta(hours=48), takes_context=True)
def expire_offer(evt: OfferMade, ctx: DeliveryContext) -> None:
    offer = Offer.objects.get(pk=evt.offer_id)
    if offer.accepted:
        return  # the world moved on: nothing to do, and that is a success
    if offer.expires_at > max(ctx.due_at, timezone.now()):
        raise NotYet(offer.expires_at, reason="the offer was extended")
    offer.expire()

    The time belongs to the receiver rather than to fire() because one event drives reactions at different times: the same OfferMade sends the offer now and expires it in 48 hours. And it is a receiver rather than a separate schedule() call because every delivery row hangs off an event, and the event is the record of why the work exists.

    Declaring a due time¶

    due= takes one of two spellings:

    Spelling Due at
    A timedelta That long after the event's occurred_at, the domain's own time
    A callable of the event The aware datetime it returns, for a time the event itself names 
    @receiver(NewsletterScheduled, due=lambda evt: evt.send_at)
def send_newsletter(evt: NewsletterScheduled) -> None: ...

    The row is written with its available_at and its due_at at the due time, so the relay claims it then and not before.

    A due time is never earlier than the row. One already past - an event fired with an old occurred_at, say, in a backfill - is clamped to the moment the row is written, the event's recorded_at, and is owed from then. due_at is when a row became owed, which cannot be before it existed: without the clamp a backfilled event would read as a backlog that old the instant its row was written, and a give_up_after measured from it would dead-letter its first deferral.

    A callable runs inside the caller's transaction

    As a targets= callable does: at fire time, beside the event row, seeing the business change's own uncommitted rows. If it raises, or returns anything but an aware datetime, fire() raises and the caller's change rolls back. A naive datetime is refused rather than read as UTC or as local time, either of which is wrong for somebody by hours. A reaction that silently never happened is the failure this package exists to rule out.

    The due time is frozen at fire time

    Like max_attempts, it is resolved once, when the event fires, and written onto the row. A deploy that changes the delay reaches the next event, not the rows already waiting. A replay resolves it afresh.

    What is refused at the decorator¶

    Declared with due= Refused because
    mode=INLINE or ON_COMMIT There is no delivery row to wait in
    eager=True An eager attempt runs at commit, and a scheduled delivery is not due then
    targets= A fan-out resolved at fire time would keep that recipient set until the row came due, weeks later, while a replay resolves targets when it runs - one receiver meaning two things
    A zero or negative timedelta A delay that is not in the future is no schedule
    Anything but a timedelta or a callable Refused with TypeError, rather than at the first fire

    A scheduled fan-out is a follow-up event. Declare the scheduled receiver without targets=, and have it fire an event of its own when it runs; a receiver of that event declares targets=, which are resolved when the work is due rather than when it was scheduled.

    @receiver(ActivityPlanned, due=lambda evt: evt.starts_at - timedelta(days=1))
def remind_attendees(evt: ActivityPlanned) -> None:
    fire(ActivityReminderDue(activity_id=evt.activity_id))


@receiver(ActivityReminderDue, targets=attendees_now, takes_context=True)
def remind(evt: ActivityReminderDue, ctx: DeliveryContext) -> None: ...

    Unchanged by a due time¶

    • site="task" works as it does for every receiver. The relay claims the row at its due time and then hands it to the task backend. Nothing relies on a queue's own delayed delivery, so the schedule survives a queue that loses messages and is visible where every other delivery is.
    • Attempts, backoff and dead letters start at the due time. A receiver failing after it came due retries on its curve and dead-letters on its budget, as any other does.
    • give_up_after measures from the due time, which is when the row became owed.
    • Lanes, leases and on_failure are what the declaration says.

    When the work turns out to be moot¶

    The world moves on between the fire and the due time: the offer was accepted, the invoice was paid, the activity was cancelled. There are two cases, and they have different answers.

    Found moot when it runs. The receiver re-reads current state, finds nothing to do and returns, which is an ordinary success. "Nothing to do" is not a different outcome from the relay's point of view - the delivery ran and its work is done. This is the case nobody announced, and it is why a scheduled receiver always re-checks.

    Known moot ahead of time. The code that accepts the offer knows the expiry is pointless. Waiting for it to run and succeed records a success for work that was never done; cancelling it is the answer, below.

    Cancelling work known to be moot¶

    from django.db import transaction
from django_domain_events import cancel_deliveries

with transaction.atomic():
    offer.accept()
    cancel_deliveries([offer.made_event_id], receiver_key="offers.expire_offer")

    cancel_deliveries settles the event's owed rows for that receiver as cancelled, so the relay never claims them, at the due time or after it. Inside the transaction it commits with the change that made the work moot, or not at all. It returns how many it cancelled and how many it could not, as {"cancelled": n, "in_flight": m}.

    • A pending row - still waiting for its due time - is cancelled.
    • A failed row - it came due, failed, and is waiting out its backoff - is cancelled too: "never runs" covers its retries.
    • A claimed row is left alone. A worker holds it and may be running the receiver now. It is counted in in_flight, and the receiver's own re-check is what stops it: cancelling does not replace the re-check, it saves the run.

    cancelled is a settled status of its own, distinct from a success (the receiver never ran) and from a dead letter (nothing failed). Health does not count it, prune treats it as consumed under either retention policy, and replay reopens it - the way back for work withdrawn by mistake.

    The event id is what fire() returned. Keep it where the work's subject lives - a column on the offer - or find it through the event log by name and payload, which is a query on the event table rather than on this package.

    An operator rather than the application has the same operation in two other places: python manage.py cancel_deliveries <event_id> --receiver <key>, and the admin's Cancel selected deliveries on the rows a status or receiver filter has already picked out. The command is covered in operations, the admin actions in the admin.

    When the work is not due yet: NotYet¶

    The early case is the dangerous one, because it fails silently. An admin extends an offer from 48 hours to 72; the 48-hour row runs, finds the offer still open, and returns - which succeeds, and leaves nothing that will ever expire the offer. Rescheduled activities, extended invoice dates and moved newsletters all have the same shape.

    NotYet(until, reason="") is the answer. The receiver recomputes the due time from current state, compares it with DeliveryContext.due_at - the time the row came due with - and raises when the work has moved later:

    if offer.expires_at > max(ctx.due_at, timezone.now()):
    raise NotYet(offer.expires_at, reason="the offer was extended")

    The clock term is there because a late run must proceed rather than fail: a row that runs after the moved time has nothing left to wait for, and NotYet refuses a time already come, so comparing against ctx.due_at alone would raise that refusal as an ordinary failure on every retry.

    The relay writes until to the row's available_at and due_at together, sets it back to pending, and does nothing else:

    • No attempt is spent, and the next run is handed the same attempt number. max_attempts cannot end it.
    • It is not capped by MAX_RECEIVER_RETRY_DELAY_SECONDS and not bounded by give_up_after. Those exist because a destination's advice about its own readiness cannot be trusted to be sane; this is the application's own declaration of when its work is due, as due= is.
    • It is not a failure: last_error is not written and on_failure is not called.
    • It sets nothing else aside. The rest of the batch is delivered.

    On the next run ctx.due_at is until, so the same comparison now lets the work go ahead. A direct deliver_one() reschedules the same way and returns PENDING, and refuses a pending row whose time has not come, as it refuses one waiting out its backoff.

    until must be an aware datetime in the future when the exception is built. A naive one is refused, and so is a time already come: spending no attempt, the row would be claimed straight back and answered the same way, a loop nothing would end. Both are refused where the exception is constructed, so a receiver raising either has raised an ordinary TypeError or ValueError, which the relay records as an ordinary failure, counted, with that message.

    Only a durable receiver can be rescheduled: NotYet is read by the relay and by nothing else. An INLINE receiver raising it fails the caller's transaction like any other exception, and an ON_COMMIT one has it logged. NotYet is not limited to receivers declared with due=, though: any durable receiver can answer "not until then", and its row is scheduled from that moment.

    Firing an event is still the cleaner reschedule

    Where the application knows the moment a due time moves - the admin action extending the offer - firing an event there and reacting to it keeps the log honest about why the time changed. NotYet is what keeps the work from being lost when that is forgotten, so the package does not depend on every change that moves a due time remembering to.

    NotYet or RetryAfter?¶

    They rest on the same machinery and answer different questions.

    RetryAfter(seconds) NotYet(until)
    Whose knowledge The destination's: "come back in two minutes" The application's: "this work is due then"
    Takes A delay An absolute time
    Spends an attempt Yes by default; not with counts=False Never
    Capped by MAX_RECEIVER_RETRY_DELAY_SECONDS Yes No
    Jittered With counts=False No
    Bounded by give_up_after With counts=False No
    Pauses the lane With counts=False No
    Recorded as failed, with last_error, telling on_failure pending, with neither

    Use RetryAfter when something outside asked you to wait; use NotYet when the work itself is not due.

    Replay¶

    replay_events resolves a scheduled receiver's due time again, through the same resolver fire() uses, and writes the later of that and now. Replay is how a scheduled receiver is adopted - declare the expiry, replay the offers made this week - and writing now would expire every open offer at once. An offer whose time has already passed is expired by the replay, owed from it. A due callable is called again, and raises to the operator on the same terms as at fire time.

    requeue_dead() makes a row due at once, as it does for every receiver: it only touches dead rows, which were due when they died.

    A cancelled row is reopened like any other settled one, at the later of its due time and now. requeue_dead() leaves it alone.

    In tests¶

    drain_outbox() skips the backoff, and never skips a schedule: a row whose due time has not come is left alone, so a test that makes an offer does not expire it in the same breath. Pass now= to move forward instead:

    def test_an_unanswered_offer_expires():
    offer = make_offer()
    drain_outbox()
    assert not offer_expired(offer)

    drain_outbox(now=timezone.now() + timedelta(hours=48))
    assert offer_expired(offer)

    now= is the moment every claim of the call is made at - what is due, which backoff has run out and which lease has lapsed are all read against it. deliver_pending(now=) takes the same. The receiver still runs on the real clock, since its acknowledgement is a fact about when it ran: compare against ctx.due_at, with the real clock only as the floor that lets a late row proceed (max(ctx.due_at, timezone.now())), and the receiver behaves the same under a test's now= as in production, because the rows a test moves forward to are due after the real clock.

    Within one call, a row is attempted at most once if its receiver answers NotYet, and handed to a task backend at most once if it declares site="task" - a handed-off row is not counted in the result, whether the backend runs it there and then or later. The pass keeps the row out of its later claims only when it was given a now; on the real clock a rescheduled row's time and a handed-off row's lease are ahead of the clock when written, so a later claim takes the row again only once that time has really come, and nothing is excluded or read back.

    Is it keeping up?¶

    A scheduled row is work that has not arrived. outbox_health() counts it as scheduled, not as owed, and never reads the backlog age from it: a reminder due in fourteen days would otherwise trip a five-minute backlog alert five minutes after it was written, and hold it open for the fortnight.

    Once it comes due, a row is owed like any other, and its age is measured from its due time rather than from its event: a reminder failing an hour after it came due is an hour behind, not a fortnight. events_status prints the scheduled total and a scheduled= figure per receiver beside owed=.

    The catalogue publishes a receiver's due time as due_seconds or due_callable, with a line under its table, so a reader sees that a reaction runs later. See the catalogue.

    A scheduled row holds its event

    A prune deletes only events with no unsettled delivery, so a row due in six weeks keeps its event for six weeks, whatever the event's retention. The work is owed, so that is the right answer; the event goes at the first prune after its last delivery settles.

    A scheduled receiver can look quiet

    quiet_receivers() reports a receiver with no success inside its window, and a scheduled receiver has none on days nothing came due. Choose within for it from its schedule.

    Nothing wakes the relay at a due time: an idle relay finds the row on its next poll, so within POLL_SECONDS of it. The NOTIFY a fire() sends on commit wakes the relay when the row is written, and it finds nothing due yet.
